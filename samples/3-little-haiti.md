### Overview

Little Haiti in Flatbush grew from a late 19th-century farming community into a major Caribbean diaspora hub, driven by a massive influx of Haitian and West Indian immigrants during the 1970s and 1980s ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/05/18/nyregion/in-central-brooklyn-a-branch-of-3d-world.html)). Over these decades, community members established supportive mutual aid networks, commercial enterprises along major corridors, and active political organizations to advocate for their rights ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/08/15/nyregion/immigrants-help-others-lost-in-maze.html); [The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/05/05/us/long-docile-haitian-americans-turn-militant.html)). ### Then & now

#### 1900-1920

During this era, Flatbush underwent a rapid transition from a rural and semi-suburban farming community into an urbanized residential neighborhood driven by the electrification of the Brooklyn, Flatbush & Coney Island Railroad in 1899 and improved rapid transit links ([LPC LP-0979, 1979](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/0979.pdf)). [...]

#### 1940

By 1940, the neighborhood building stock shifted from freestanding suburban homes to large brick elevator apartment buildings constructed along major transit corridors like Flatbush, Ocean, and Nostrand Avenues ([Prospect Park South Historic District Report](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/0979.pdf)). [...]

#### 1960

Researchers searched for mid-century demographic shifts, the arrival of West Indian and Haitian immigrants, and the physical and commercial appearance of Flatbush in 1960, but did not find any documented records. (unverified)

#### 1980

During the 1970s and 1980s, a massive influx of Caribbean and international immigrants settled into the neighborhood, leading the press to describe Central Brooklyn and Flatbush as a "branch of 3d World" ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/05/18/nyregion/in-central-brooklyn-a-branch-of-3d-world.html)). Haitian-Americans established active mutual aid networks and community advocacy organizations to help newcomers navigate housing, legal, and social systems ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/08/15/nyregion/immigrants-help-others-lost-in-maze.html)). By the late 1980s, Haitian-Americans became increasingly organized and politically active, transitioning into a visible, militant constituency advocating for their community's rights ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/05/05/us/long-docile-haitian-americans-turn-militant.html)). Local commercial corridors like Flatbush Avenue served as active hubs for immigrant-owned businesses, though community expansion also occasionally sparked commercial tensions and boycotts ([The New York Times, 1990](https://www.nytimes.com/1990/05/19/nyregion/woman-sues-boycotted-grocery-in-flatbush.html)). [...]
